When the Internet Starts Acting for Us

How AI agents are shifting the web from something we browse and operate to something we increasingly delegate.

Imagine you need a new washing machine. You search, compare retailers, check dimensions and delivery dates, and find out whether someone will remove the old one. The information is available, but you assemble it and operate each service yourself.

Now imagine asking: “Find three reliable machines under £600 that fit this space, can arrive this week and include removal of the old one. Show me the options before buying anything.”

The outcome may be similar. What changes is who works through the process. A person describes the intention and constraints; an agent chooses steps using the services available to it.

This is a useful direction to investigate, rather than a claim that one assistant can already complete every example below. Available tools, permissions and supported services determine what can actually happen.

Websites have capabilities beneath their screens

A retailer provides search, filters, product pages and checkout. Beneath those interfaces, its systems can search a catalogue, check stock, calculate delivery and create an order. A restaurant system can check availability and reserve a table.

People usually reach those capabilities through an interface. Software can also request them through an API: a structured connection between systems. Where an authorised agent has access to the right connection, it need not reproduce every click a person would make.

MCP, the Model Context Protocol, provides a common way for AI applications to connect to tools and data. It describes available operations and their inputs and results. It does not itself supply a business's stock database, booking service or complete security system. Those capabilities still have to exist and be connected. MCP documentation

This gives a website a second audience. People need clear information and useful interfaces. An agent needs accurate information, usable capabilities and enforceable permissions.

From finding an option to completing a task

Consider a hotel request: somewhere near tomorrow's meeting, with parking, accepting dogs and costing less than £180. An assistant may help gather and compare information, but completing a booking also requires current availability, the final price, cancellation conditions and authority to commit.

Being able to describe a suitable hotel is different from being able to book it. Search, connected tools and transactions are separate capabilities, and their availability varies between platforms and services.

Existing commerce protocols show how those connections can be made. The Universal Commerce Protocol describes operations for checkout and related commerce services; its specification is a foundation for integrations, rather than proof that every retailer is already accessible to every agent. Universal Commerce Protocol

For a business, the useful question is increasingly specific: can an authorised assistant check a fact, obtain a quote or complete a supported action, and then verify the result?

The task may continue after the transaction

A confirmation screen usually ends an online purchase. An agent could be given a longer responsibility: watch the delivery and alert the customer if the date changes.

If a washing-machine delivery moves to a time when nobody is home, the assistant might offer to request a different slot. That example requires access to delivery updates, relevant calendar information and a way to contact the supplier. Changing the arrangement would also require the customer's authority.

Some infrastructure already supports work that continues over time. Vercel's Workflow documentation, for example, describes durable workflows that can pause, resume and wait for external events. That makes ongoing processes possible; it does not automatically make them reliable personal assistants. Workflow documentation

The design problem is to preserve the purpose of a task while conditions change. What should the system monitor? When should it ask? When has it finished? How does someone withdraw the responsibility?

Personal context needs a clear boundary

“Find somewhere I would actually like for dinner” asks the assistant to combine a live situation with personal preferences. Location, dietary requirements, walking distance and current table availability may all matter.

That does not mean every restaurant needs the person's underlying conversation or history. The assistant might use private preferences to compare options, then send the chosen restaurant only the information required for a reservation.

At SR3H, this is one question behind Brane: which context improves a task, which information stays private, and when should the assistant use no extra history at all? Retaining information, using it and sharing it are separate decisions. Who owns the context?

Delegation needs enforceable limits

An agent that can read a menu has different responsibilities from one that can send an email, cancel a booking or spend money. Instructions to be careful are insufficient on their own.

The services underneath must establish who is making a request and what that requester may do. An assistant might be allowed to compare products but require approval to purchase, or make a reservation only when it is refundable. Spending limits, time limits and access to personal information need explicit boundaries.

MCP's authorisation specification describes access controls for its HTTP transport. Product-specific rules still need enforcement by the systems carrying out the work. MCP authorisation specification

People also need a usable account of what happened: which service was contacted, what was shared, what commitment was made and whether the intended outcome was confirmed. Failure, cancellation and correction belong in the design from the start.

What businesses can prepare now

The first step is accurate, accessible information about the offer: capabilities, limitations, prices where appropriate, service areas and contact details. Established search foundations remain relevant. Google says its AI search features require no additional technical optimisation beyond those foundations. Google Search Central

Beyond discovery, choose a small action worth making available to an authorised assistant. Checking availability or requesting a quote can be a more useful starting point than exposing every operation at once. Define the inputs, permissions, possible failures and evidence of completion.

This connects with our work on Signal and Conchup. Signal investigates how AI finds and presents businesses; Conchup keeps requirements, decisions and evidence connected as software changes. Each addresses part of the distance between an intention and a result that someone can review.

People will still browse shops, explore holidays and enjoy using applications directly. Delegation adds another choice. The important work is deciding which responsibilities software can usefully take on, what information it needs and where the person remains in control.
