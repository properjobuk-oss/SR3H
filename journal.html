23 September 2026 Everyone wants a better answer. Better for whom? AI discovery can serve customers and businesses at once. That does not make their interests identical. Read postClose post

AI discovery can serve customers and businesses at once. That does not make their interests identical.

A customer asks an AI to find the right supplier. A supplier asks how to become the recommended answer.

A good match satisfies both. A capable business that goes undiscovered is a missed opportunity for the customer too.

AI visibility concerns whether and how a business or its content appears in AI-generated answers. It might be named, cited as a source, compared with alternatives or recommended. These are different outcomes: being used as a source does not necessarily mean being recommended as a supplier. Google for Developers

Answer engine optimisation (AEO) and generative engine optimisation (GEO) describe efforts to improve that visibility. The important question is whether improving visibility also improves the answer. Google for Developers

Imagine a specialist whose website says little beyond “bespoke solutions”. It starts explaining the materials it works with, typical costs, delivery times and projects it can handle.

The company wants to reach more customers. By explaining what it actually does, it also gives an assistant better information for matching it with people who need those services. The two aims can support each other without being identical.

Its own website is only part of the picture. AI search can also draw on reviews, product listings and discussions elsewhere, bringing other accounts of the business into consideration. Google for Developers

An assistant could take this further than a results page: clarify what someone needs, compare evidence and explain why a less prominent supplier deserves consideration.

The underlying friction is that the business wants to be chosen, while the customer needs the assistant to remain willing to choose someone else.

Suppose that specialist publishes a six-week minimum lead time. An assistant could use this to recommend it for a planned project—and rule it out for someone needing delivery in three weeks. Fewer recommendations could mean better matching and fewer wasted enquiries.

This complicates what counts as success. Microsoft’s AI Performance dashboard reports page-level citation counts, explicitly distinguishing them from importance, ranking or placement. Those counts reveal something about visibility. On their own, they cannot establish whether a customer received better advice. Bing Blogs

If appearances become the target regardless of suitability, the incentive changes. Explaining what a company actually does could give way to making it appear relevant to as many questions as possible. Google already warns against producing pages for numerous query variations primarily to manipulate rankings or AI responses. Google for Developers

The distinction is not simply whether something is marketing, but whether it gives the customer reliable grounds for a decision. A paid introduction could reveal an excellent option. Payment itself is not evidence that the option fits.

If an assistant loses these distinctions, the customer may have to do the filtering again: checking commercial relationships, verifying claims and looking beyond the suggested shortlist. The answer may be shorter without the decision becoming easier.

From being found to getting things done

The same question becomes more consequential when the assistant can act.

Instead of searching, comparing, exchanging emails and placing an order, someone might ask their device: “Find a suitable supplier, arrange it within this budget and let me know when it is done.”

Some of the foundations remain familiar. Clear descriptions, accurate business details and pages that search engines can crawl and index still matter. Google’s AI search features rely on its search index; special AI files are not a substitute for that foundation. Google for Developers

Beyond being read, a business can make its systems available for an assistant to use. MCP—the Model Context Protocol—provides a standard way for AI applications to access tools and data, such as a catalogue or an availability check. Model Context Protocol

Where a business operates its own agent, A2A, or Agent2Agent, provides a way for compatible agents to communicate. The business can publish an Agent Card online: a machine-readable description of its agent’s capabilities and how to contact it. This supports discovery between compatible systems, not automatic inclusion in every assistant’s recommendations. A2A Protocol

The customer might see a quote or booking form inside the conversation. MCP Apps already support interactive interfaces in compatible AI applications. These visible cards are different from the machine-readable Agent Cards used between agents. Model Context Protocol

Connected to the right systems, a company’s agent could field enquiries, collect missing details, prepare quotes and arrange bookings. Commerce protocols also provide mechanisms for checkout, payment integration and fulfilment updates. These are capabilities to implement, not features a business acquires merely by publishing a card. What works depends on the systems connected and the capabilities they support. Google Developers Blog

Delegation would still need clear limits on spending, commitments and when approval is required. It would also need a way to resolve mistakes or failed fulfilment. “Let me know when it is done” should reduce the customer’s work, not remove their control.

Making a business easier to deal with could help both sides. But it introduces another version of the original question: could an assistant favour a supplier because it is easier to transact with, rather than because it better meets the customer’s needs?

Ease of purchase can be part of a good match. It should not become a substitute for one.

This is the wider question of AI mediation: how an assistant helps someone choose—and act—in a market trying both to serve them and to influence them.

Commercial involvement could make that encounter more useful, not less. The test is whether better information and better connections help a business get chosen and help the assistant recognise when it should not be.

23 September 2026 Big Brain AI Monster Seeing behind the chat: the many separate processes that turn a question into one smooth answer. Read postClose post

Seeing behind the chat.

You press Send. A confident answer appears. It is easy to imagine a vast intelligence somewhere, considering your question. Look behind the interface, though, and the picture becomes rather less cinematic.

Imagine your question leaving Oxford, passing through London and reaching a computing centre near Dublin. These locations are illustrative. The application adds selected conversation history and instructions, and the work may branch: a search request to Virginia, a document fetched from Frankfurt, a calculation performed in Ireland. Separate services do separate jobs, sometimes simultaneously. Not every question needs every branch. Anthropic

Your question has become several messages carrying different parts of the work. Some information travels across an ocean. Some barely leaves the building.

Inside the language model, the transformer is another sequence of operations. Words and word-fragments become lists of numbers. Calculations give some parts of the available text more influence than others. Those contributions are combined, passed through further calculations and combined again. Successive layers repeat this pattern. The numerical settings governing these operations were adjusted during training—not individually written as rules, and not normally rewritten while answering your question. Google Research

No mysterious mental landscape is required to picture this. Numbers enter an operation; new numbers come out and feed the next. The work can be divided across tightly connected chips, each calculating a portion and passing on its results. NVIDIA GitHub Page

Eventually, these calculations produce scores for possible next words or word-fragments. In shorthand: X is a weak fit. Y scores better. P scores better still. These alternatives are scored numerically, not debated by a little voice. A selection procedure chooses a fragment—not always the highest-scoring one—and the process continues with that addition included. The surrounding context shapes each prediction, not merely the previous word. And “likely continuation” does not mean “verified fact”.

This is where other tools can make the difference between plausible language and useful work.

A search can retrieve an actual source. A database can return a stored record. A calculator can work out a figure. A code tool can run the program rather than merely produce something that looks like one. The model generates a request; other software performs the operation; the result comes back as new input. Predicting an instruction and carrying it out are different jobs. OpenAI Platform

The results from Virginia, Frankfurt and Ireland return to the continuing process. A calculation may replace an unsupported figure. A retrieved passage may supply missing evidence. A failed test may trigger a revision. Output becomes input. Branches separate and rejoin. The language model generates further text from this changed starting point, perhaps requesting another operation before answering you. OpenAI Platform

Around it sits plenty of conventional software. Some checks really are as ordinary as: Required information missing? Request it. Wrong format? Reject it. Retry limit reached? Stop. Less an awakening creature; more a substantial amount of admin moving extraordinarily quickly. Anthropic

Better training, more relevant information, clearer tool instructions and useful tests can each improve the result. These improvements accumulate across the system. But they must be tested: another step can correct a mistake, or simply give it another outing. More machinery is not automatically better machinery. Anthropic

And some important work happened before anything left Oxford. You supplied the subject, direction and constraints. Your question has already narrowed the field. The system is not beginning with every conceivable problem and discovering yours from scratch. Your corrections narrow it further; the next response is generated from the revised input. Anthropic

That makes the exchange partly a mirror. Your premise can return better organised and more persuasive without having been independently tested. Learned agreeableness can make the reflection particularly flattering: “Excellent point.” Sometimes that is a pleasing conversational continuation, not the conclusion of a rigorous examination. Excessive agreement is a documented failure, not a certificate of your brilliance. OpenAI

The combined system can still perform sophisticated work, including reasoning and coordination. Those capabilities belong to the organised processes. They do not establish an additional someone sitting above them, reading every result and directing proceedings.

By the time the reply reaches Oxford, a great deal of routing, retrieving, multiplying, weighting, selecting, testing and predicting may have happened. The separate operations arrive on your screen as one smooth reply.

Then the interface says: “I think.”

21 September 2026 Is AI visibility optimisation just SEO dressed up? What AI visibility work shares with SEO, what is genuinely different and what still needs evidence. Read postClose post

Much of the work is familiar: clear product pages, accessible websites, reviews, comparisons and coverage elsewhere. Google says established SEO practices apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode, with no special markup or additional technical requirements. Google Search Central

OpenAI similarly describes product information, reviews and third-party content among the inputs that can shape shopping results. OpenAI Help Center

Updating a website or securing press coverage can be useful. Calling it AI optimisation does not, by itself, make the method new or more effective.

Third-party coverage still matters. Research published by AI-visibility platform Peec AI, based on nearly 200,000 AI responses, found that higher positions in frequently cited comparison lists were associated with better brand visibility. Additional placements showed diminishing returns, and results varied between markets and systems. These were associations, not proof that securing a placement causes more recommendations. Peec AI

This raises a familiar concern. Businesses with the resources to keep publishing and securing coverage may shape more of the material an assistant encounters. Promotional claims could then reappear as an apparently independent recommendation. That is a risk worth examining, rather than evidence that the biggest marketing budget always wins.

The more interesting difference is how the customer’s need develops.

Someone asks for a laptop, then explains their budget, frequent travel and dislike of fan noise. Each detail changes what would make a suitable recommendation. ChatGPT can use the conversation and relevant saved preferences to shape product selection and searches. The recommendation is being made against a developing situation, not just a category name. OpenAI Help Center

Assistants can also turn a question into several searches, then search again after reading the results. Google calls this query fan-out; OpenAI says ChatGPT may rewrite a request into one or more targeted queries and refine them after reviewing initial results. An answer may therefore draw on information the customer never explicitly asked to find. Google Search Central · OpenAI Help Center

That suggests more useful tests than counting mentions. Does the assistant find a business without being given its name? Does it describe the offer correctly? Does the recommendation remain suitable as the customer adds constraints? Being praised when named does not establish that a business would have entered consideration unaided.

The questions used for testing matter just as much. Adding “which product should I buy?” to an informational question steers the answer towards a purchase. It cannot establish that the original need would naturally lead there. Nor does testing thousands of invented questions reveal how often real customers ask them.

Some findings expose less obvious problems. In controlled tests with fictional companies and web search disabled, changing the company name altered how several models described otherwise comparable businesses. Occasionally the description followed the meaning of the name rather than the supplied facts. This is evidence of a weakness in interpretation, not a demonstrated strategy for improving sales. Peec AI

Discovery can now lead directly to action. Supported shopping and reservation services can connect recommendations to purchases or bookings. Here, discoverability includes whether the assistant can help complete the task, rather than simply describe a possible supplier. Availability depends on the platform and service. OpenAI Help Center

These differences deserve investigation. They do not make every new dashboard or content recommendation an advance. More mentions need not mean more suitable customers. A rise after a website update does not establish that the update caused it.

This is the distinction we are exploring through AIDO Labs: not simply whether a business appears, but whether AI understands when it is genuinely relevant and brings it into the conversation for the right reasons.

For now, the practical foundations remain close to SEO, content marketing and PR. The stronger claim—that optimisation can improve how accurately an assistant matches a business to someone’s actual need—requires separate evidence. The question is whether this work improves that judgement, or mainly gives the publicity industry another way to influence it.

12 September 2026 When buyers ask AI, who gets the work? How AI decides when a business is relevant, why suitable suppliers can be overlooked and what businesses can do to improve discovery. Read postClose post

An architect asks an AI assistant how to put an early price on a house extension. The assistant might offer a rough figure, recommend a specialist estimating service, or use a connected tool to produce an estimate within the conversation. Whether an outside business becomes involved depends on the response.

This is an easy part of AI discovery to miss. Before a supplier is brought in, the conversation has to reach a point where its help is needed. Someone asking what an extension might cost could be satisfied with a general answer. Someone needing an estimate from drawings has given the assistant a more specific problem, which might lead it towards a specialist.

For businesses, that raises a question: under what circumstances does AI bring us into the conversation? AI discoverability optimisation (AIDO) means helping AI find a business, understand what it offers and select it when it fits the need.

Search engine optimisation still provides much of the groundwork. Pages need to be accessible and useful, with clear information about what a business offers. Google says its existing SEO principles also apply to its AI search features.

Being absent from an answer can have several explanations. The assistant may have missed the business, misunderstood what it does, or found something better suited to the request. It may have provided enough help itself. Each possibility calls for a different response, so counting mentions alone leaves much of the story unexplained.

There is a revealing test here. Ask an assistant to suggest services for a particular job. Then introduce a business it left out and ask whether it could help. It may assess the business favourably once named. That suggests it could have been overlooked, and gives us something to investigate. The assessment still needs checking against what the business can actually deliver.

At SR3H, these questions are shaping AIDO Labs, our work on demand intelligence: understanding where customer needs create opportunities for businesses. We start with the situations that lead people to seek help, then repeat questions across AI systems, with different context and at different times. This helps us see which patterns hold: when AI brings in a specialist, who it considers and where a suitable option seems to be missing. Understanding how often real customers encounter those situations requires further evidence.

The next step is to investigate what could improve the outcome. Perhaps a service needs a clearer explanation, stronger evidence, or a way for AI to use it directly. Sometimes the product itself needs to change. We record what changed and repeat the tests, with enquiries, trials and purchases helping us judge whether it made a useful difference.

The opportunity may begin several questions before anyone asks for a supplier. Understanding those earlier questions could change both how a business explains its work and what it chooses to offer.

10 September 2026 Who owns the context? Brane, local AI and the emerging layer between people and models. Read postClose post

A small computer on a desk can now do something that, until recently, belonged in a data centre.

Apple’s new Mac mini uses the M6, its first 2-nanometre chip. It combines a 12-core GPU, dedicated neural hardware and up to 32GB of unified memory, which the processor and graphics system can draw from as one pool. Apple explicitly presents it as a machine for running secure, private AI work locally. Apple

On that machine, a suitably compressed version of Qwen3.8‑27B sits within practical reach. Qwen’s open-weight model has 27 billion parameters, can work with text, images and video, supports tools and extended reasoning, and has a native context window of more than 260,000 tokens. Its official release reports substantial gains in coding, research and multi-step agent work. It can be run through open software rather than a metered cloud API. Hugging Face

That does not make the computer free. The hardware, electricity, setup and maintenance still exist. Nor is there good evidence yet that a local Qwen model matches GPT‑5.6 Sol or Codex across the hardest, longest and most complicated jobs. Some routine coding and professional tasks may feel surprisingly close; difficult end-to-end work will still expose the difference.

But the boundary has moved. A model capable of useful reasoning, coding, visual understanding and tool use can now run entirely in a room, with no per-token charge and no requirement for the prompt, source code or private documents to leave the machine.

At almost the same moment, conversational context has become visibly valuable in another way.

OpenAI now offers advertising in ChatGPT. With personalised ads enabled, selection can draw on the current conversation, past chats and memory. OpenAI says those conversations and memories remain inside ChatGPT and are not disclosed to advertisers, who receive aggregated performance information rather than personal chat histories. The distinction matters. This is not evidence that conversations are being sold. It is evidence that a detailed understanding of someone’s interests, circumstances and present intention has commercial value. OpenAI Help Center

One development makes personal context increasingly practical to process privately. The other makes its economic value more explicit.

The important question is no longer only how intelligent an AI model can become. It is who owns the information that makes that intelligence useful.

Brane is an SR3H Lab project exploring that boundary. Its working proposition is to give a model the smallest safe amount of context that measurably improves its answer, while keeping the richer personal and project state under the user’s control.

That sounds simple until it meets the capabilities of a current frontier model.

GPT‑5.6 Sol already has a context window of more than one million tokens. It can search the web and files, use computers, discover tools, call MCP services and coordinate work across several stages. OpenAI says it can write and run small programs that choose tools, process intermediate results and decide what to do next. A claim that Brane is innovative merely because it “chooses useful context” would therefore be weak. The model and its surrounding platform are already doing sophisticated versions of that. OpenAI Developers

This is the uncomfortable but useful test of the idea. If GPT‑5.6 Sol already understands the task, searches the right sources and remembers the right details, what is Brane for?

The answer may be that Brane is not another reasoning layer inside the model. It is the user’s side of the system.

A frontier model can decide how to reason over the information available to it. It cannot independently decide which private sources should have been made available in the first place. It cannot protect material that has already been sent to it. It cannot guarantee that a project decision is current rather than merely similar to the question. It cannot provide continuity across competing model providers unless that continuity exists outside them. Nor can it give the user an independent, inspectable account of which personal information was disclosed and why.

Brane’s possible role sits before the main inference. It would maintain a user-owned state of evidence, decisions, relationships, permissions and history, then decide what temporary view—if any—a particular model should receive.

That makes it less like a second brain competing with GPT‑5.6 and more like a private control plane around whichever model is best for the task.

The distinction becomes important because memory is not automatically beneficial.

An old project decision may be highly relevant and completely wrong. A preference may still be true but inappropriate in the present role. A previous solution may create continuity while preventing a better idea from appearing. A system can become more personalised and, at the same time, more repetitive.

Memory can preserve understanding. It can also preserve error.

This is one of the more interesting parts of the Brane enquiry. A useful memory system should not only know how to retrieve. It should understand supersession, decay and contradiction. It should know that a later accepted decision may replace an earlier one without erasing the historical record. It should recognise that an unconfirmed personal inference becomes less trustworthy with time. It should be able to expose two conflicting records rather than quietly averaging them into a false certainty.

It should also be able to abstain.

For an open design question, a piece of personal history might make the answer more familiar but less original. Continually reminding a model of previous tastes, products and conclusions could gradually turn it into an echo of the user’s past. A genuine novelty safeguard would sometimes leave relevant history out, not because the system had failed to find it, but because a new answer needed room to form.

Relevance is not the same as authority. Authority is not the same as usefulness.

The correct result may occasionally be no added context at all.

This is where the familiar technical labels become less important. RAG retrieves material when a request arrives. CAG keeps a limited body of stable knowledge ready for reuse. A knowledge graph preserves relationships that ordinary text similarity may miss. MCP gives a model a standard way to call live tools and services. Each is useful, but none is a complete description of the product.

The more durable layer is the policy that decides which approach is appropriate now.

Research is already moving in this direction. Adaptive-RAG uses a smaller model to choose between no retrieval, simple retrieval and a more involved process according to the question. Response-Aware User Memory Selection goes further by estimating how a memory would change the answer, rather than selecting it only because its wording resembles the request. LongMemEval found that commercial assistants and long-context models suffered a substantial accuracy drop when required to maintain information across extended, changing interactions.

Brane has not invented these component ideas. Its possible value lies in combining them around a different ownership model, then testing whether the combination produces a worthwhile improvement.

The calculation is not simply whether a memory is relevant. It is closer to a balance:

Expected improvement, uncertainty removed and useful continuity must outweigh staleness, privacy exposure, delay, token use and the risk of narrowing the answer.

The first version need not involve an elaborate new model. Much of the decision could begin with clear rules. Is the request personal or generic? Is it factual or exploratory? Does the active project contain an accepted constraint? Is the information recent? Does it conflict with anything newer? Is the local machine able to complete the work without disclosure?

A local model could handle the less mechanical parts: searching private files, identifying contradictions, compressing a project state, masking sensitive values and estimating whether a piece of history is likely to help. The final task could remain local when that is sufficient. When it is not, Brane could send a small, permission-checked packet to GPT‑5.6, Codex or another specialist system.

The value of the local model is therefore not limited to trying to beat the frontier model.

It can decide when the frontier model is necessary, prepare better material for it and reduce what must cross the boundary.

For an individual, this could mean keeping personal history, messages and documents on their own hardware. For a company, it could mean that product plans, source code, commercial assumptions, internal discussions and client material are searched and organised locally, while only the minimum necessary abstraction is sent to an external model.

That does not remove every privacy concern. A local knowledge store is itself a sensitive asset and needs strong security, deletion, export and access controls. A hybrid system still requires trust in any external service it uses. But it changes privacy from a broad promise into a series of concrete computational decisions: what stayed local, what was transformed, what left the machine and why.

This work also connects with a wider line of enquiry inside SR3H.

Across Brane, Conchup, Red Thread and Clarity, a related design problem keeps returning: how to turn scattered, incomplete and changing human input into a state that is current enough to trust and structured enough to improve the next output.

Conchup approaches this through requirements, authority, provenance and accepted product state in software development. Red Thread explores useful association across material that would otherwise remain fragmented. Clarity examines how private personal patterns might support bounded reflection without becoming intrusive. Brane applies the same broad concern to the boundary between a person and an AI model.

These are not yet one finished platform, and it would be misleading to describe them as one. What they share is a research direction.

The quality of an AI-assisted product often depends less on the final generation than on the condition of the information entering it. A coding model working from an obsolete requirement can produce excellent code for the wrong product. An estimate built from incomplete assumptions can be precise without being useful. A personal assistant can remember every conversation and still misunderstand which parts remain true.

Better output often begins further upstream.

Brane could become a practical test bed for that idea across SR3H’s work: not a universal memory attached indiscriminately to every product, but a way of testing whether governed state, selective context and local processing improve software, estimates, decisions and workflows.

The standard of proof needs to be demanding.

Brane should not be tested against an artificially forgetful chatbot. It should be tested against GPT‑5.6 Sol working normally, with the platform’s existing memory, tools and long context. The same real tasks should be run with no additional context, a broad profile, conventional retrieval and a minimal packet selected by Brane.

The comparison should include more than factual accuracy. Did the answer apply the current constraint? Did it avoid an obsolete decision? Did it feel appropriately continuous without becoming over-personalised? Did it retain originality? How many private details were exposed? How many tokens and tool calls were added? Would a blind reviewer actually prefer the result?

Individual layers should earn their place. If a novelty safeguard makes no difference, it should not remain because it sounds intelligent. If a local model adds delay but makes the same selections as three simple rules, the rules are better. If GPT‑5.6 already handles the task perfectly, Brane should stay out of the way.

That is the more interesting version of the project. Not a claim that every answer needs more history, but an attempt to discover when context has positive value and when it becomes a liability.

The present moment makes that enquiry unusually practical. Capable open models can now run on modest personal hardware. Frontier cloud systems are becoming more autonomous and more context-aware. Conversational history is becoming commercially significant. The technical and economic pressures are moving in opposite directions: towards richer profiles inside platforms, and towards more powerful private computation outside them.

Brane sits in the space between those movements.

Its strongest possibility is not that it remembers more than the model. It is that it allows the person to retain authority over what is remembered, what is current, what may be shared and what should be allowed to fade.

The next improvement in AI may not come from placing an entire life inside a larger context window. It may come from a quieter discipline: knowing what to reveal, what to withhold, and when the most intelligent use of memory is to leave some space unknown.

8 September 2026 The road we meant to take Why navigation should help people follow the journey they chose, not quietly replace it with a faster one. Read postClose post

We would get back onto the route, only for the satnav to send us another way. After a trip of constant rerouting, we came home with an idea that became Good Route.

The frustration was simple. We wanted help following a journey we had chosen. Instead, we kept having to work out what the navigation was doing and how to get back to our plan.

For a motorcycle ride, that matters. A particular road might be the reason for the trip: the bends, the landscape, a village along the way. Arriving sooner can mean missing what you went out to enjoy.

Modern navigation is remarkably good at answering “How do I get there?” Giving it a satisfactory answer to “Which way would I like to go?” is more complicated.

Part of the difficulty sits beneath the familiar map. GPS helps a device establish its position. The navigation software then works out which road it is on, calculates a route and gives instructions. These are separate tasks. Knowing where you are does not tell the software what matters to you.

Apple Maps and Google Maps bring those tasks together so smoothly that we rarely notice the distinctions. Google describes using navigation data to reflect traffic conditions and suggest faster alternatives. That is useful when you want to reach an appointment or avoid a growing queue. On a leisurely ride, a saving of a few minutes may carry much less weight. Google Maps Help

Developing Good Route starts with that difference in priorities. The aim is to help someone follow their chosen journey, including when things stop going to plan.

A missed turning is a useful example. Should the app take you back, find somewhere sensible to rejoin, or calculate a fresh route to the destination? Each could be the right answer. It depends on what you would miss.

A point placed on a map might mark a café where friends are waiting. It might simply be there to make the route follow a particular road. Treating both as compulsory stops can produce instructions that make sense to the software but very little sense to the rider.

This is where an apparently straightforward app becomes an interesting design problem. The route needs to carry enough of the rider’s intention to make those decisions useful, without turning planning into an elaborate setup exercise.

The same restraint matters on the road. Instructions need to be clear at a glance, and changes need to be understandable. Every extra prompt asks for attention from someone who already has something more important to do.

CarPlay brings another consideration. It provides access to supported navigation apps through a vehicle’s screen and controls; the app still supplies the routing decisions. Apple Maps and Google Maps can both appear there, but a familiar dashboard does not make their behaviour identical. Apple’s CarPlay overview

For Good Route, these questions need testing through actual journeys. A convincing route on a development screen is only a starting point. What matters is how the guidance behaves at an awkward junction, after a deliberate detour, or when a rider carries on past a suggested turn.

The trip that started this gave us a specific frustration to work on. It also raised a broader question about helpful software: how does it adapt to changing circumstances while respecting a decision someone has already made?

Sometimes the longer road is the one we intended to take.
